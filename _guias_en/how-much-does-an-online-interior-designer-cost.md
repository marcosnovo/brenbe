If you are thinking about hiring help to decorate your home, you have probably run into a handful of titles: interior designer, decorator, design consultant, stylist. And a question almost nobody answers directly: how much does it cost? This guide sorts out the differences, explains how online professionals usually charge and leaves you with a list of questions to ask before you hire anyone.

We won't list specific prices here, because they vary widely by professional, region and scope, and any number we quoted could mislead you. What we can do is explain how the pricing works so you can compare quotes sensibly.

## What each professional does

Titles are used loosely, and there is no single definition that applies everywhere. As a general guide:

- **Interior designer.** Plans the layout and design of a space: floor plans, traffic flow, lighting, materials, color and furniture. On projects with construction, an interior designer may coordinate with architects, contractors and trades. In some US states, "interior designer" is a regulated title, so credentials can vary by location.
- **Decorator or design consultant.** Focuses on the finishing layer: color, furniture, textiles, lighting and styling. Typically works without construction, which is where most online services sit.
- **Stylist.** Concentrates on the final look: arranging objects, art, plants and textiles, often for photos or staging.
- **Online or e-designer.** Not a separate profession but a format: the work is done remotely, through photos, measurements, questionnaires and video calls, instead of in-person visits.

None of these titles is automatically better. The right fit depends on whether your project needs construction coordination (then look for a designer or architect licensed where you live) or a plan for decorating what you have (then a decorator or e-design consultant often does the job).

## How online interior design is priced

There are three common models. Many professionals mix them.

### Flat fee per room or per project

You pay one price for a defined scope: for example, one room, with a set number of deliverables such as a layout, a palette and a shopping list. This is the most common e-design model.

- **Pros:** you know the cost upfront and can compare packages.
- **Watch for:** what exactly is included (revisions, number of calls, shopping lists, a mood board) and what counts as extra.

### Hourly rate

You pay for the professional's time, usually in a call or a block of consulting hours. It suits specific questions: "which of these two sofas?", "what color should I paint this room?", "does this layout work?"

- **Pros:** flexible, good for small questions and for people who want to do most of the work themselves.
- **Watch for:** how time is counted (does prep time count?), a minimum number of hours, and a clear estimate so the bill doesn't grow unexpectedly.

### Package or tiered pricing

A designer offers two or three levels, for example a basic consultation, a design plan and a full plan with a shopping list. The more detailed the deliverables, the higher the tier.

- **Pros:** easy to choose a level that matches your needs and budget.
- **Watch for:** compare tiers by what you receive, not by their names.

Some designers also earn money through retailer discounts or commissions on products you buy. That isn't necessarily a problem, but it should be disclosed.

## What affects the price

Even within the same model, quotes can differ a lot. The main factors:

- **Scope.** One room costs less than several, and a quick color question costs less than a full plan.
- **Deliverables.** A mood board is less work than a detailed layout, a shopping list with links and a styling guide.
- **Revisions and support.** How many rounds of feedback are included, and whether you can ask follow-up questions after delivery.
- **Experience and portfolio.** Professionals with more experience or a specialized niche may charge more.
- **Location and market.** Rates differ across countries and cities. Working with a designer based in another country can change the cost structure, and some clients find that it adds value, while others prefer a local professional for in-person needs.
- **Product sourcing.** Whether the designer simply recommends pieces or also orders them for you.

## Questions to ask before you hire

1. **What exactly is included?** Ask for the deliverables in writing: layout, palette, mood board, shopping list.
2. **How many revisions are included?**
3. **How does the process work and how long does it take?** Calls, deadlines, how you share photos and measurements.
4. **Do I buy from you or from any retailer?** Ask about markups or commissions.
5. **Can you work with what I already own, and with my budget?**
6. **What isn't included?** Construction oversight, installation and structural advice are usually separate.
7. **How is payment handled and what is the refund policy?**
8. **Can I see examples of similar projects?**
9. **What credentials or licenses apply here?** If your project needs a licensed professional by local rules, confirm this early.

## How to get the most from an online consultation

- **Prepare your photos and measurements.** Shoot each wall, plus the room at different times of day.
- **Set a realistic budget range.** Even a rough range helps the recommendations be practical.
- **Gather inspiration.** A few images you love, and a few you don't, say a lot.
- **Be clear about what you want to keep.** An heirloom or a sofa you love changes the plan.
- **Ask about next steps.** Find out how you will use the plan once the consultation ends.
