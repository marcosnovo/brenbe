Choosing living room paint colors usually starts with browsing photos and ends in front of a wall covered in forty swatches that all look the same. To avoid that paralysis, it helps to have a method: first understand your room's light, then decide on a base color, and only then choose accents. This guide gives you that order.

## Start with light: window direction and time of day

The same color looks different depending on how much natural light enters and from which direction. In the US, which is in the northern hemisphere, here is a handy rule of thumb:

- **South-facing living room:** it gets warm, bright light for most of the day. Almost any color looks good, and you can handle deeper or cooler shades. Very saturated colors can feel intense in strong sun.
- **North-facing living room:** it gets softer, cooler, more even light. Warm, light tones tend to work better and keep the room from feeling chilly. A deep color can also work if you embrace the moodier feel.
- **East-facing living room:** bright, fresh morning sun, then calmer light in the afternoon.
- **West-facing living room:** warm, strong afternoon and evening light. Colors can look more yellow or orange at that hour.

Visit your living room at different times before deciding. If you spend most of your time there in the evening, artificial light will matter more than daylight, and colors will shift under warm versus cool bulbs.

## Warm neutrals: a base that works

If you want a safe foundation, warm neutrals are the easiest place to start:

- **Warm whites and creams** keep things bright without the sterility of a stark white.
- **Sand, beige and greige** add depth and pair well with wood and natural fibers.
- **Soft taupes and mushroom tones** feel calm and modern.

Look at the undertone, not just the name. A cream with a yellow undertone looks very different from one with a pink or green undertone, especially next to your flooring and sofa. Hold the swatch against your biggest, least changeable pieces first.

## Greens: easy on the eye

Green is a natural fit for living rooms because it sits comfortably with wood, plants and natural light.

- **Sage and soft olive** feel relaxed and work in almost any light.
- **Deep forest or bottle green** looks rich and enveloping, especially with warm lamps and wood furniture.

Greens pair well with cream, walnut, brass and rust-toned accents.

## Terracottas and earthy warm tones

Clay, terracotta, rust and warm brown add warmth and character. They work best when you want a cozy, lived-in feel.

- Use a **muted terracotta** on an accent wall or in textiles if you are not ready to commit to a full room.
- Pair with **cream, olive and natural wood** to keep it balanced.
- Avoid very orange tones in a room with strong west light, where they can feel overwhelming.

## Blues: calm, with range

Blue is flexible, from soft and airy to dark and dramatic.

- **Pale, gray-leaning blues** feel fresh and calm, and work in bright rooms.
- **Navy and deep teal** add drama and make a good backdrop for light furniture and warm metals.

In a north-facing room, cool blues can feel cold, so balance them with warm textiles, wood and lamps.

## Dare to go dark: when it works

A deep color doesn't have to shrink a small room. Dark walls blur the corners and can make the space feel wrapped and intentional, especially in rooms used mostly in the evening. To make it work:

- Use **good lighting** at several heights, with warm bulbs.
- **Paint the trim and ceiling** in the same color, or a very close tone, for a seamless look, if you're comfortable with a bolder move.
- **Repeat the color** in a smaller way elsewhere, such as in a cushion or a piece of art.
- **Always test a large sample** first.

If you rent, check your lease before painting and consider options like removable wallpaper or a deep-colored large rug and curtains to get a similar effect.

## The 60-30-10 rule

A simple way to build a balanced palette:

- **60%** a base color, usually on walls and large surfaces.
- **30%** a secondary color, in upholstery, rugs or curtains.
- **10%** an accent, in cushions, art, lamps and small objects.

Natural materials such as wood, stone, rattan and metal also count as colors, so include them when you plan. Stay within three to five colors in total and the room will read as one cohesive space.

## How to test paint properly

1. **Use a big sample.** Paint at least 2 by 2 feet (about 60 by 60 cm), or paint a board and move it around.
2. **Test on more than one wall.** Light changes from wall to wall.
3. **Look at it all day.** Morning, afternoon and night, with and without lamps on.
4. **Check it next to your furniture and floors.** The color has to live with what you already own.
5. **Wait a day or two.** First impressions can change once you have seen it in different light.

Remember that paint finish matters too: flat or matte finishes hide imperfections and look soft, while eggshell or satin finishes are easier to clean. Check what is recommended for living room walls and trim in your area.
