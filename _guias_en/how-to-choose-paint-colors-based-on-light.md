The paint color you see on a swatch or in a photo is never exactly the color you'll see on your wall. What changes between the two is **the light**. So before you think about trends, look at how the sun enters each room, at what hour and with what temperature. This guide gives you a method for choosing paint color room by room, with criteria that hold in any house or apartment in the US.

## Step 1: find out which way each room faces

In the northern hemisphere the sun rises in the east, arcs across the **south** and sets in the west. That's why a south-facing window is the brightest one and a north-facing window gets the softest. To find your bearings, stand at the window and use your phone's compass.

| Direction | What light it gets in the US | How color behaves | Where to start |
|---|---|---|---|
| **North** | Soft, cool, indirect light with little direct sun, even all day | Cool colors look muted and neutrals can turn gray | Warm, light tones: cream, sand, peach, or a deep color with good lamplight |
| **South** | The brightest, warmest light for most of the day, and deeper into the room in winter when the sun is lower | Colors look rich and generous; very saturated ones can tire with so much sun | Almost any color family; greens, blues and neutrals stop looking cool |
| **East** | Bright, fresh morning sun | Beautiful in the morning; in the afternoon the room drops in tone | Tones that don't go cold in the afternoon: soft greens, creams |
| **West** | Strong, warm afternoon and evening sun; hot in summer | Everything looks more yellow-orange at sunset | Cooler or neutral tones to balance, and avoid very warm saturated colors |

Two warnings: light **changes with the seasons** (in winter the sun sits lower and reaches deeper into a room), and **buildings, trees and awnings** can change everything. Watch your room at different times of day and in different seasons before you decide.

## Step 2: understand the temperature of light

Natural light shifts through the day: cooler at midday and dawn, warmer at sunset. **Artificial light** has a temperature too, measured in Kelvin (K) and printed on the bulb's package:

- **2700 K:** warm, yellowish light. Typical for living rooms and bedrooms.
- **3000 to 3500 K:** warm-neutral, good for kitchens and bathrooms without feeling "clinical".
- **4000 K or higher:** cool, white light. Useful in work areas, but it hardens almost any color.

With the same paint, a 2700 K bulb and a 5000 K bulb look like two different colors. **Paint for the light you'll actually live with**: if you use the room mostly at night (often the case for a living room or bedroom), artificial light rules.

## Step 3: use the light to your advantage, room by room

- **Living room:** we spend a lot of time there in the evening and at night. Prioritize a color that looks good in warm light. To go deeper on this room, see our guide to [paint colors for a living room]({{ '/en/guides/best-paint-colors-for-a-living-room/' | relative_url }}).
- **Bedroom:** soft, low-saturation colors favor rest; if it faces east, morning light can wake you, so a deeper tone helps.
- **Kitchen:** it needs good light and easy cleaning. Light, warm tones with a satin finish are usually practical.
- **Bathroom:** if there's no window, avoid colors that depend on daylight; artificial light and the reflection off the tile rule.
- **Hallways and entryways:** often dark. A light, warm color helps, or a deep, decisive tone if you want a "jewel box" effect.
- **Ceiling:** a warm off-white usually works better than a stark white, which can look harsh and show patches of light.

## A palette to start with

The palette above is designed for a room with little natural light (north-facing, or with neighbors close by). For the other two typical cases, here are different starting points (always check them with a sample):

**Bright south-facing room**

| Name | Hex | Suggested use |
|---|---|---|
| Bone white | #F2EEE6 | Base (walls and ceiling) |
| Sage green | #9AA58B | One wall, doors or furniture |
| Ink blue | #2F3B52 | Accents: pillows, a framed piece |

**Afternoon room facing west (balancing the heat)**

| Name | Hex | Suggested use |
|---|---|---|
| Warm pearl gray | #DAD6CE | Base |
| Muted sea-glass green | #A9BDB3 | Secondary wall or textiles |
| Dark wood | #5A4636 | Furniture and details |

These are tones chosen as references: the same hex looks different across brands, batches and walls. If you want to use them, take these references to a paint store and ask for the closest match.

## Step 4: test before you paint

This is the step almost everyone skips, and the one that prevents the most mistakes:

1. **A large sample.** At least 2 x 2 feet (60 x 60 cm), with **two coats**, on white poster board or on the wall (with a white border around it to see the true color).
2. **Try it on several walls**, not just the brightest one. The color changes on each.
3. **Look at it in the morning, in the afternoon and at night**, with the lights on.
4. **Wait for it to dry.** Many paints shift a shade as they dry.
5. **Compare it next to what you already have:** floor, furniture, curtains, tile. Undertones show up only there.

## Finishes: flat, satin or gloss

The finish changes how light bounces and how much the imperfections show:

| Finish | Effect | Where it's used |
|---|---|---|
| **Flat / matte** | Doesn't reflect; hides imperfections; deeper color | Ceilings, living room and bedroom walls |
| **Satin** | Slight sheen; holds up better to cleaning | Kitchens, bathrooms, doors, baseboards, trim |
| **Gloss** | Very reflective; shows every flaw | Small accents, woodwork |

A wall with raking light (a window right beside it) shows imperfections more, so go with flat and good wall prep there.

## Common mistakes

- Picking the color from your phone screen or a magazine photo.
- Testing with only a 2-inch (5 cm) paper chip.
- Painting a north-facing room a cool gray and expecting it to look luminous.
- Forgetting about the light at night.
- Mixing many different whites (ceiling, baseboards, doors) without checking that their undertones get along.
