Renting doesn't have to mean living somewhere that doesn't feel like yours. Most of what makes a room look and feel good (lighting, textiles, layout, plants, order) needs no renovation and no permission. This guide covers what is usually allowed, what's worth checking and how to build a plan in stages, so that what you buy today also works in your next apartment.

This is general decorating guidance, not legal advice. Your lease and your landlord decide what you can change.

## What you can do and what to check first

Every lease is different, and this guide doesn't replace reading yours: **review it and, if in doubt, ask your landlord or a legal professional.** As a general guide, anything you can do without leaving a trace usually lands on the "free" side, and anything that alters the apartment (even a little) lands on the "ask first" side:

| Almost always fine | Better to ask first |
|---|---|
| Rugs, curtains, pillows, throws | Painting walls or doors |
| Floor and table lamps | Drilling holes with wall anchors |
| Your own furniture, rearranging what's there | Replacing light fixtures or faucets |
| Plants, pots, art leaned on a surface | Removing or swapping furniture that came with the unit |
| Organizing and adding storage | Wallpaper with permanent adhesive |

A practical tip: even if your lease doesn't require it, **send a message about what you plan to do and keep the reply**. It prevents misunderstandings when you move out.

## Priorities by budget

It doesn't matter how much you have to spend: the order is the same. First what changes daily life the most, then what beautifies.

1. **Declutter and clear.** Free, and it changes the most. Remove what you don't use and group what's left.
2. **Lighting.** Warm bulbs and at least two or three low light sources (floor lamps, table lamps or plug-in sconces).
3. **Large textiles.** A rug, curtains, a coverlet or a throw for the sofa.
4. **One star wall.** With removable wallpaper, art or a large mirror leaned against it.
5. **Plants and objects.** Last, once you know what's missing.

## Removable fixes that work

### Lighting without installation

Rentals often come with one harsh overhead light and nothing else. Three changes that need no work: a **floor lamp** by the corner of the sofa, **table lamps** on a shelf or nightstand and **warm-white bulbs** (around 2700 K) in all of them. There are also plug-in wall sconces, and you can tidy cords with adhesive cord channels that peel off when you leave. If your ceiling has a simple socket, a shade that hangs from the cord without touching the wiring can be an option, but confirm with your landlord first.

### Curtains

A long curtain changes how tall a room looks. Hang it **as close to the ceiling as you can** and let it reach the floor. Tension rods and suction-mounted rods avoid drilling; for heavy fabrics, look for rods that rest on the window frame without screws, or ask whether you can use the existing holes.

### Peel-and-stick wallpaper and wall coverings

Removable wallpaper is a very popular solution, but it isn't magic:

- Test a hidden piece and wait a few days before peeling it off.
- Choose smooth, dry, clean walls; on damp walls or old paint it doesn't stick well.
- On smaller surfaces (the front of a dresser, a closet, kitchen cabinet doors) the risk is lower.

### Rugs

A large rug is probably the best removable investment: it defines zones, covers flooring you don't like and adds warmth. Make it big enough that the front legs of the sofa and armchairs sit on it. A rug pad underneath prevents wrinkles and protects the floor.

### Art, mirrors and shelves

Lean large pieces on a dresser or straight on the floor, overlapping: it's a look in its own right and needs no holes. Mirrors leaned against a wall spread the light and add depth. For lightweight frames, removable adhesive strips solve a lot, as long as you respect the weight limit on the package.

### Plants

They add life and texture with almost no work. If you don't get direct light, pick hardy indoor species. Group them at different heights on stools, shelves or hanging pots on a curtain rod.

### Furniture

Think about pieces that adapt to different layouts: modular shelving, lightweight tables, medium-sized sofas. Rearranging what you already own (turning the sofa, moving the table) counts as decorating too.

## One simple rule: what moves with you

Before you buy, ask yourself: **will I take this with me when I leave?** If yes, it's an investment; if not, it should be cheap or truly necessary. That way your money turns into a home you can set up anywhere.
